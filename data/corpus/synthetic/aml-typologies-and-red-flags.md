# Money laundering typologies and red flags for remittance and FX businesses

Provenance: a plain-English summary written for this project, drawing on themes in publicly available AUSTRAC and FATF typology publications. It is simplified and is not legal advice.

## Structuring (smurfing)

Structuring, also called smurfing, is breaking a large amount of cash into smaller transactions below the AUD 10,000 threshold to avoid a threshold transaction report. Common signs are repeated cash transactions just under AUD 10,000, deposits made at several branches on the same day, and several people depositing cash for the same customer.

## Cuckoo smurfing

Cuckoo smurfing exploits international remittances. A person overseas sends a legitimate transfer to a beneficiary in Australia, but instead of the funds arriving through the remitter's normal channel, criminals deposit cash proceeds of crime into the beneficiary's account. The beneficiary usually does not know their account has been used. Red flags include cash deposits by third parties into an account that is expecting an international transfer, and deposits split across locations in structured amounts.

## Money mules

A money mule is a person who receives funds into their account and moves them on for someone else, often after answering a job advertisement or online contact. Signs include a new or dormant account that suddenly receives several payments and quickly transfers them out, often internationally.

## Trade-based money laundering

Trade-based money laundering disguises criminal proceeds as payments for goods or services. Signs include invoices that do not match market prices, goods described vaguely, and payments from third parties with no link to the trade.

## Red flags at the FX counter and in remittance

- A customer is reluctant to provide identification or gives inconsistent details.
- Transactions do not fit the customer's known profile, occupation or stated purpose.
- A customer asks how to avoid a report, or asks staff to split a transaction.
- Funds are moved rapidly through several accounts or remitters with no clear purpose.
- Frequent transfers to or from jurisdictions identified as high risk.
- Large cash amounts in used or small-denomination notes with no plausible source.

A single red flag does not prove wrongdoing. Red flags are a reason to look more closely, record what was observed, and escalate under the escalation procedure.
